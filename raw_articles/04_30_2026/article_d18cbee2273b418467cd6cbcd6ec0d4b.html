Iran Conflict Drives Jet Fuel +84%, Threatens Summer Flights

Photo by:   regional gateaway
Share it!
By Teresa De Alba | Jr Journalist & Industry Analyst - Wed, 04/29/2026 - 15:07

European airlines are facing rising operational costs and network disruptions as the conflict involving Iran drives a sharp increase in jet fuel prices and constrains key transit routes. Since Feb. 28, prices have risen nearly 84%, placing sustained pressure on airline margins ahead of the summer travel season. Carriers are adjusting pricing, capacity and procurement strategies as uncertainty over fuel availability and cost volatility persists across global markets.

Willie Walsh, Director General, International Air Transport Association, said to Reuters that the sector could face supply limitations despite current stability. 

“There is a risk that we’ll see rationing of fuel supply, particularly in Asia and Europe,” says Walsh, noting that supply “remains robust for now.” He also emphasized that evolving geopolitical conditions are increasing risk exposure for airlines and fuel suppliers.

Walsh contrasted the current environment with the pandemic period, highlighting differences in demand dynamics. 

“I think COVID was on a completely different scale,” says Walsh. “What we’re seeing here is, in effect, a cost issue for the airlines. The underlying demand for aviation remains robust, and that’s a positive.” His assessment indicates that while passenger volumes remain stable, profitability is being affected by rising input costs rather than a structural decline in travel demand.

Fuel costs and hedging strategies under pressure

Airlines have partially mitigated the increase in fuel costs through hedging strategies, which allow them to lock in prices for future consumption. However, these financial protections are beginning to expire, exposing carriers to spot market volatility. As hedges roll off, airlines face higher procurement costs, creating uncertainty in earnings guidance and fare-setting strategies during peak travel periods.

The conflict has disrupted global energy flows, particularly through the Strait of Hormuz, a critical corridor for oil and gas shipments. Reduced traffic through this route has contributed to supply constraints and price increases, affecting energy markets and airline operations. The disruption has also influenced investor sentiment, with airline shares reflecting increased exposure to fuel price fluctuations and geopolitical risks.

Policy responses and industry perspectives on supply risks remain mixed. Ebba Busch, Sweden’s Minister for Energy, issued an “early warning” regarding potential jet fuel shortages and advised travelers to reassess travel plans

For its part, Michael O'Leary, Chief Executive, Ryanair, says the immediate risk of supply disruption appears to be easing. “We think the risk of a supply disruption is receding,” he said, citing discussions with fuel suppliers across Europe.

Diverging airline performance and capacity adjustments

Major European carriers, including Air France-KLM, International Airlines Group and Lufthansa, have responded by increasing fares and reducing capacity. These measures aim to offset rising operating costs while maintaining financial performance during the summer travel period, which typically represents a significant share of annual revenue.

Data from Cirium Ascend indicates that Middle Eastern carriers have been more directly affected by route disruptions. Flights operated by regional airlines declined 50% year-on-year in March, while bookings for the second and third quarters through Gulf hubs fell 42.5%. Despite these declines, global passenger capacity remains approximately 2% higher in 2026 compared with 2025, suggesting that overall demand has not materially weakened.

Some carriers have benefited from shifting travel patterns. Finnair reported increased demand on Asian routes, reflecting rerouting trends and changing passenger preferences. Norwegian Air Shuttle indicated limited exposure to supply risks, highlighting variations in business models and geographic exposure across the sector.

Government authorities are beginning to respond to rising travel costs. Jordi Hereu, Spain’s Minister of Industry and Tourism, urged consumers to book tickets early to mitigate exposure to future price increases. He said current fares still reflect earlier fuel purchases but are expected to rise as airlines adjust to higher kerosene costs. 

“What we are recommending is that people buy their tickets now because it is true that airlines are currently using kerosene that was purchased some time ago, and therefore there’s an element of price fluctuation involved,” says Hereu.

Hereu added that “it is already clear that prices have risen and this could affect demand,” pointing to early signs of reduced booking activity. Global oil prices have increased by approximately 50% since Feb. 28, following military actions involving the United States and Israel targeting Iran. Reuters analysts estimate that long-haul fares from Europe could increase by more than US$100 per ticket as airlines pass on higher costs to consumers.

The International Energy Agency reported that oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz have declined to approximately 2 million barrels per day, compared with about 20 million barrels per day before the escalation. The agency identified reopening the route as a key factor in stabilizing supply and reducing price volatility in global energy markets.

US to Lift Eritrea Sanctions as Red Sea Tensions Reshape Alliances

The US is set to lift sanctions on Eritrea to secure Red Sea trade routes. This pivot from human rights to realpolitik reshapes the Horn of Africa's future.

SO
Streamline OfficialVerified
May 6, 2026(edited)Reading

In a major shift in Washington’s Africa policy, the United States has announced plans to lift several long-standing sanctions against Eritrea. The decision, which marks a thawing of relations between the West and the "North Korea of Africa," is driven by the urgent need for stable partners along the Red Sea, a vital maritime corridor currently under threat from Houthi rebels and regional instability.

Eritrea has been under various forms of international sanctions for over a decade, primarily due to its involvement in regional conflicts and its human rights record. However, the changing security landscape—specifically the disruption of global trade through the Bab el-Mandeb strait—has forced a pragmatic recalculation in the U.S. State Department.

The Red Sea Security Calculus

The Red Sea carries approximately 12% of global trade and 30% of the world’s container traffic. With the Suez Canal seeing a 40% drop in revenue due to security threats, the U.S. is seeking to build a "Red Sea Coalition" that includes littoral states. Eritrea, with its over 1,000 kilometers of coastline and strategic ports like Massawa and Assab, is an indispensable player in this strategy.

Diplomatic sources indicate that the lifting of sanctions is contingent on President Isaias Afwerki’s government providing "logistical cooperation" for international naval patrols. It also hinges on the complete withdrawal of Eritrean forces from Ethiopia’s Tigray region, a demand the U.S. has maintained since 2021. The move is a classic example of "realpolitik," where human rights concerns are balanced against global economic security.

Economic Opportunities for Asmara

For Eritrea, the removal of sanctions could unlock billions in frozen assets and open the door for foreign direct investment, particularly in the mining and energy sectors. The country sits on significant deposits of potash, gold, and copper, which have remained largely untapped due to the restrictive financial environment.

  • Potash Projects: The Colluli Potash Project, estimated to have a mine life of 200 years, could become a major global supplier if financing is restored.
  • Port Rehabilitation: Investors from the UAE and China have already expressed interest in modernizing the Port of Assab, which could serve as a secondary gateway for landlocked Ethiopia.
  • Regional Integration: Lifting sanctions would allow Eritrea to rejoin the Intergovernmental Authority on Development (IGAD) as a full, active member.

A Risky Bet on a Hermit State

Critics of the policy warn that Washington is rewarding an autocratic regime without securing meaningful internal reforms. Human rights organizations point to the mandatory indefinite national service and the lack of a constitution as evidence that Eritrea remains a repressive state. There is also the "spoiler" risk—Eritrea’s history of shifting alliances means any partnership could be short-lived.

In Nairobi, the move is seen as a mixed blessing. While a stable Eritrea benefits East African trade, there are fears that an emboldened Asmara could reignite old border disputes with Djibouti or Ethiopia. Kenya’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs is reportedly monitoring the situation closely, emphasizing that any lifting of sanctions must be part of a broader regional peace framework. As the U.S. pivot continues, the Red Sea is becoming the new theater of the Great Power competition, with Eritrea as its most unlikely protagonist.

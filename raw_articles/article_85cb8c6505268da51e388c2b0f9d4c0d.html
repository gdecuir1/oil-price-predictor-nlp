Dangote’s Tanga refinery idea needs ‘corridor finance’

In mid-April in Nairobi, Aliko Dangote did what he often does: dazzle the markets. But what would it take for the necessary feedstock and structures to be in place to make the 650,000bpd regional refinery bankable?
Published on April 28, 2026 at 12:00 pm (GMT +1)

Before a crowd of bankers and corporate bosses gathered in Nairobi on 23 April, the Nigerian industrialist and billionaire Aliko Dangote sketched out a 650,000 barrel-a-day (bpd) refinery at Tanga on Tanzania’s coast. The project would echo his Lagos refinery, but with a different logic. Rather than serve one giant domestic market and feed on massive national oil reserves, it aims to anchor an East African fuel system, drawing crude from several countries and reducing exposure to global shocks.

The ambition should be taken seriously.

East Africa imports too much refined fuel. Consumers, transport operators and governments remain exposed to conflicts, currency swings and shipping bottlenecks – as the Iran war demonstrates too well. A regional refinery could deepen industrial capacity, create a more resilient energy base and give governments more room for manoeuvre. Dangote has the credibility to force that conversation.

Is a Tanga refinery financially viable?

But scale is not the same as bankability. The question is not whether Tanga is desirable. It is how such a project can be made financeable without turning a strategic idea into a stranded monument.

A refinery depends on a port, pipelines, storage, power, roads, customs systems, distribution networks and political agreements across borders

A refinery is not bankable because a region needs petrol. It is bankable when lenders can see a reliable crude supply, predictable tariffs, enforceable offtake agreements, foreign-exchange arrangements and a cost of capital low enough to make the numbers work. In East Africa, that means what I have christened “corridor finance”.

Because of patchy infrastructure, projects that look like single platforms end up behaving like corridors. And in a corridor project, the asset is never just the asset. A refinery depends on a port, pipelines, storage, power, roads, customs systems, distribution networks and political agreements across borders.

It is like multiple jugglers tied at the wrists. If one falters, the whole performance unravels. Lenders intuitively price the correlation among risks.

Establishing synchronicity across obstacles

That correlation creates a “synchronisation premium”. A refinery may look viable on paper when evaluated on its merits. But once revenues depend on several governments, regulators, currencies and pieces of infrastructure moving together, the weighted average cost of capital can quickly rise.

At a mid-tens cost of capital, even strong cash flows can be thinned out. The project may still make strategic sense, but it will not close on slogans.

This is where Dangote’s seriousness matters. His Lagos refinery showed that African refining on a vast scale is not a fantasy. It also showed how hard such projects are inside one national system, with one sovereign, one dominant market and a business group able to carry extraordinary financing risk.

Despite massive political capital, a huge home market and vast oil reserves, it still took a decade and 2.5x the budget. Tanga is extra hard because it is regional from the start.

Test 1: Limited crude availability

Feedstock is the first test. A 650,000bpd refinery running at 90% capacity needs about 585,000 barrels of crude every day. Uganda’s Lake Albert production is expected to plateau at around 230,000bpd, but Hoima has first call on part of that supply.

Kenya’s Turkana fields remain constrained by export infrastructure. South Sudan’s crude moves through Sudan, where war makes reliability a central question. The DRC’s oil geography does not neatly solve Tanga’s supply problem.

READ MORE Black gold, dead cattle: The displaced search for answers in Uganda’s oil zone 

None of this makes Tanga impossible. It means the sovereignty story has to be sharpened. The refinery may still need imported crude, including from the Middle East, at least for a long period. That need not kill the project. Refineries often blend crudes and manage diverse supply books.

But the pitch should shift from full insulation to reduced vulnerability and more regional value capture.

Test 2: Lack of infrastructure

The second test is infrastructure. The East African Crude Oil Pipeline was conceived mainly as an export route for Ugandan crude to the coast. Turning that wider system into a refinery-feeding architecture would require tariff redesign, third-party access rules, storage integration and credible commitments from governments and multiple operators.

A larger regional refinery will need a different operating model from the outset

It would require what in corridor finance we call “operator syndication.” These are not technical footnotes. They are the project.

Uganda’s two-decade struggle to get the smaller Hoima refinery over the line is a warning. If a 60,000bpd plant with one main sovereign counterparty can remain trapped in investor exit cycles and asset synchronisation blues, a larger regional refinery will need a different operating model from the outset.

Corridor financing requires policy stamina

For Tanga, that may mean phasing. A first module of perhaps 150,000-200,000bpd would test supply, logistics and offtake before the full edifice is built. It may mean political-risk guarantees, foreign-exchange backstops, shared tariff principles and a corridor charter with binding dispute-resolution mechanisms.

These instruments are dull. They are also what make ambition cheaper.

READ MORE 5 questions to understand why the Nigerian oil sector has turned a corner

Yes, Tanga is a serious strategic idea. Yes, Dangote has earned the right to be heard when he talks about African refining. And yes, if the region wants the project to move from announcement to final investment decision, it must now do the unglamorous work of corridor finance.

African industrial sovereignty will not be delivered by photographs, communiqués or billionaires alone. It will be delivered when ministers, regulators, pipeline operators, lenders and refiners make risk legible. That takes “policy stamina”.

If Tanga succeeds, by escaping the katanomic trap, it will be because the politics was organised well enough for the policy maths to work.

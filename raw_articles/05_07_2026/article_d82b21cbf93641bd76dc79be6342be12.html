Gas Prices Today: AAA Average Hits $4.536 as Oil Slides

Gas prices are sending two different signals at once. The pump is still painful: AAA's daily tracker puts the U.S. regular-gas average at $4.536 on May 6, up 30.7 cents from a week earlier and $1.378 above the year-ago level. The oil market, though, is starting to price in a possible break in the Iran-Hormuz crisis after Reuters reported that Brent and WTI futures fell sharply on signs the U.S. and Iran were moving closer to an initial agreement.

That split is the whole story for drivers. Pump prices rose because crude, gasoline inventories, diesel supply and summer-grade fuel rules all tightened at once. Relief is possible if oil stays down, but it will not show up instantly on the station sign.

What Changed in the Last 24 Hours

The latest headline looks contradictory: gasoline got more expensive while crude oil got cheaper. AP reported that U.S. regular gasoline climbed to about $4.54 on Wednesday and was roughly 52% higher than before the Iran war began. The same day, AP also reported that oil prices sank on hopes the Strait of Hormuz could reopen more fully.

The contradiction disappears once the timing is separated. Retail fuel is a delayed price; futures are a forward-looking price. The pump reflects crude and wholesale gasoline that already moved through the system, while the futures market is trying to price what supply may look like two or three weeks from now.

Gas Prices Today: The Real Numbers

The best daily read is AAA, while the best weekly government read is the U.S. Energy Information Administration. They do not match perfectly because they measure different days. AAA shows regular gasoline at $4.536, mid-grade at $5.015, premium at $5.388 and diesel at $5.674 as of May 6. EIA's latest weekly survey shows regular gasoline at $4.452 for May 4, up 32.9 cents from the previous week, and on-highway diesel at $5.640.

The daily jump is not small. AAA's national regular average moved from $4.483 on May 5 to $4.536 on May 6, while the week-ago number was $4.229 and the month-ago number was $4.119. That means the pump has absorbed most of the April crude shock even as futures started selling off on May 6.

The State Map Is Brutal

The national average hides a huge regional gap. On AAA's state average page, California is at $6.160 for regular gasoline, Washington is at $5.747, Hawaii is at $5.657, Oregon is at $5.332 and Nevada is at $5.233. At the low end, Oklahoma is at $3.962, Mississippi is at $3.970, Louisiana is at $3.993, Arkansas is at $4.005 and Texas is at $4.043.

California is not just above the national number. AAA's California page shows regular at $6.160 and diesel at $7.503 on May 6, with Los Angeles-Long Beach at $6.241 and San Francisco at $6.360 for regular. AAA's Oklahoma page shows the other end of the market: regular at $3.962 and diesel at $5.030, with Oklahoma City at $3.986 and Tulsa at $3.993.

The difference comes from crude logistics, refinery configuration, state fuel rules and taxes. The West Coast is structurally more expensive because it is less connected to Gulf Coast refining supply, while the Gulf Coast and nearby inland states usually get cheaper fuel faster. BTS reported that the average April regular-gas price was $5.38 on the West Coast but $3.71 on the Gulf Coast, a spread big enough to shape consumer budgets and freight costs.

Why Prices Are Still High Even as Oil Slid

The May 6 oil selloff matters, but it does not erase the supply damage already in the system. Yahoo Finance's WTI futures feed showed WTI at $96.17 near the May 6 close, down from a previous close of $101.94. Brent futures were at $102.24, down from $108.17, and RBOB gasoline futures were at $3.3264, down from $3.5952.

Those futures moves point toward pump relief if they hold. But EIA's Weekly Petroleum Status Report shows why the immediate setup is still tight: U.S. commercial crude inventories fell by 2.3 million barrels to 457.2 million barrels in the week ended May 1, total motor gasoline inventories fell by 2.5 million barrels to 219.8 million barrels, and gasoline stocks were 4% below the five-year average. Refinery utilization was 89.6%, gasoline production averaged 9.810 million barrels per day, and four-week motor-gasoline product supplied averaged 9.0 million barrels per day.

The second pressure point is diesel. EIA's weekly report shows diesel at $5.640 for May 4 and distillate fuel inventories 11% below the five-year average. That matters beyond trucking companies because diesel is embedded in freight, agriculture, construction and wholesale distribution.

Hormuz Is Still the Center of the Story

The Strait of Hormuz is not a normal shipping lane. EIA's world oil chokepoints data shows 20.9 million barrels per day of crude oil and petroleum liquids moved through Hormuz in the first half of 2025. IEA's Hormuz factsheet says roughly 20 million barrels per day of crude and oil products moved through the Strait in 2025, equal to about 25% of world seaborne oil trade, with only limited pipeline capacity available to bypass it.

That is why a headline about ships moving again can hit oil prices within minutes. Reuters reported that oil fell on May 6 as optimism grew around a possible U.S.-Iran agreement, but the same report noted that normalizing flows can take weeks even if access improves. A partial reopening can cool futures faster than it cools retail gasoline.

The April EIA forecast is still a warning label. In its Short-Term Energy Outlook global oil section, EIA said Brent averaged $103 in March, reached almost $128 on April 2, and was expected to average $115 in the second quarter before easing later in 2026 under its conflict-resolution assumptions. In the petroleum-products section, EIA forecast U.S. regular gasoline at $3.70 for 2026 and diesel at $4.80, both well above 2025 averages.

The Summer Blend Is a Real Constraint

Even without geopolitics, May and June are bad months for gasoline supply. EPA's Reid Vapor Pressure rules require summer gasoline sold to consumers from June 1 through September 15 to meet lower volatility limits, with many regulated parties starting earlier. Lower-volatility fuel helps reduce evaporative emissions in hot weather, but it also narrows the supply pool during the spring transition.

That is why prices often rise before Memorial Day and do not fall as quickly as crude. When refineries are already dealing with maintenance, blend changes and lower inventory, the retail market can stay elevated even after futures take the first leg down.

What It Means for Inflation

This is already a macro story. The March CPI report showed the energy index up 10.9% in March and the gasoline index up 21.2% for the month, with gasoline accounting for nearly three quarters of the monthly all-items CPI increase. That was before AAA's national average pushed through $4.50 in early May.

For investors following TECHi's March CPI breakdown, the key question is whether April and May gasoline prices turn into a broader inflation impulse or remain a headline-energy shock. The Federal Reserve's April 29 FOMC statement still described inflation as somewhat elevated, so another hot energy print would complicate the rate-cut narrative even if core categories are calmer.

The Household Math

The pump number becomes more useful when it is tied to actual driving. FHWA's 2024 vehicle-mile table shows light-duty short-wheelbase vehicles averaged 10,812 miles traveled, 423 gallons consumed and 25.6 miles per gallon. At AAA's $4.536 national average, that fuel use implies about $1,919 per vehicle per year before any local premium, toll, parking or insurance cost.

The year-over-year hit is large. Using AAA's year-ago regular-gas average of $3.158 against the same FHWA 423-gallon benchmark, the annual fuel bill would have been about $1,336. That is roughly $583 more per vehicle at today's national average. A two-car household with normal driving patterns feels that difference quickly.

This is why diesel matters even for people who never buy diesel. When EIA shows diesel above $5.60 and distillate inventories below normal, higher freight costs can move into groceries, retail goods, building materials and delivery fees. Gasoline hits the commuter first; diesel hits the shelf price later.

What Drivers Should Do Next

The first move is not panic filling. It is shopping the spread. Gasoline prices can vary sharply within the same metro area when wholesale costs are moving quickly, so checking AAA, station apps or map-based station prices before filling up can save more than waiting for the national average to turn.

The second move is cutting wasted fuel, not comfort. FuelEconomy.gov says proper tire inflation can improve mileage by 0.6% on average and up to 3% in some cases, while the Energy Department says aggressive driving can reduce mileage by 15% to 30% at highway speeds and 10% to 40% in stop-and-go traffic. At $4.536 gasoline, those percentages are not trivia.

Drivers who can shift one commute day, combine errands, or move a long road trip by a week may benefit if the May 6 oil break holds. Premium fuel is another avoidable leak for many households: FuelEconomy.gov's octane guide says most gasoline vehicles are designed to run on 87 octane, while higher octane is required only for vehicles whose manufacturer calls for it. The key is that pump relief, if it comes, is likely a one-to-two-week story rather than a same-day story.

What Would Make the Price Break Lower

Three things need to happen together. First, WTI should hold below $100 and Brent should stay near or below the low-$100s after the first flush of deal optimism. Second, RBOB gasoline futures need to avoid rebounding back toward the May 5 close. Third, EIA needs to show that gasoline inventories are stabilizing rather than drawing again.

If those conditions line up, the price cut should show up first at wholesale terminals, then at high-turnover stations, then in the AAA national average. If any one of those conditions fails, the national average can stay pinned near $4.50 even while headlines say oil is down.

What Investors Should Watch

Energy stocks are not a simple "buy because gas is expensive" trade anymore. High crude and wide refined-product margins can support producers and refiners, which is why TECHi's best oil stocks guide focuses on balance sheets, dividends and refining exposure rather than just crude beta. But a real Hormuz deal can pull the war premium out of oil quickly, and the fastest winners can become the fastest profit-taking targets.

The better dashboard is five numbers: AAA regular gasoline, RBOB gasoline futures, WTI, EIA gasoline inventories and EIA diesel inventories. If RBOB stays below $3.35 and WTI stays below $100, the odds improve that AAA's daily average starts easing. If gasoline inventories keep falling while diesel stays above $5.50, the consumer economy still has a fuel problem even if crude looks calmer.

For a cleaner oil-market read, TECHi's oil price today tracker is the direct companion to this post. For the cross-asset effect, the broader stock market today page is where the fuel shock shows up in equities, rates and volatility.

The Bottom Line

Gas prices probably peaked for the week if the May 6 oil break sticks. That is the good news. The bad news is that AAA's $4.536 national average is already the highest pump bill most drivers have seen since the 2022 shock, and the EIA data still show falling gasoline inventories, tight diesel supply and strong seasonal demand.

The practical read is simple: expect relief before relief is visible. Futures moved first, wholesalers move next, stations move last. If Hormuz flows normalize and RBOB holds lower, drivers should see cheaper prices later in May. If the diplomacy fades or inventories draw again, $4.50 does not become the ceiling. It becomes the floor.

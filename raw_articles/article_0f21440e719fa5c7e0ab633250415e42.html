Nigeria’s Hormuz crisis exposes deep fault lines in national energy security

Damsana Ranadhiran
  • Update Time : Sunday, May 10, 2026

For decades, Nigeria has occupied a paradoxical place in the global energy system. Despite being Africa’s largest oil producer and possessing enormous hydrocarbon wealth, the country remains vulnerable to fuel shortages, rising petrol prices, and external market shocks. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz in early 2026 exposed these long-standing weaknesses with alarming clarity.

The Strait of Hormuz is one of the world’s most strategic waterways, carrying nearly 20% of global oil trade through a narrow corridor linking the Persian Gulf to international markets. When geopolitical tensions disrupted shipping routes in March 2026, Brent crude prices surged above $114 per barrel, the highest level since 2022. The shock triggered global concern, especially for economies heavily dependent on imported fuel and petrochemical products.

For Nigeria, however, the crisis was more than an international energy disruption. It revealed deep structural failures within the country’s own energy system.

The contradiction at the center of Nigeria’s economy is striking. Although the country produces millions of barrels of crude oil, it has spent decades importing refined petroleum products because of inadequate domestic refining capacity. Fuel scarcity, long queues at filling stations, and unstable pump prices have become routine realities for ordinary Nigerians.

The launch of the Dangote Refinery in 2024 initially raised hopes that this cycle would finally end. With an initial refining capacity of 650,000 barrels per day and plans for future expansion, the refinery was celebrated as a landmark industrial achievement capable of transforming Nigeria’s energy sector. Many viewed it as a symbol of economic independence and industrial ambition.

Yet the refinery quickly encountered the same structural problems that have long undermined Nigeria’s energy industry.

International oil companies operating in Nigeria continued prioritizing exports to global markets rather than supplying domestic refineries. As a result, local refiners struggled to secure stable crude supplies despite operating in one of the world’s leading oil-producing nations. In a deeply ironic situation, the Dangote Refinery was forced to import crude oil from the United States and other African producers while Nigerian crude continued flowing abroad.

When the Hormuz crisis pushed oil prices sharply upward, these contradictions became impossible to ignore.

Petrol prices across Nigeria surged from roughly ₦870 to nearly ₦1,300 per litre within weeks. Although prices later eased slightly, the sudden increase inflicted heavy economic pain. Transportation costs rose dramatically, food prices accelerated, and many small businesses struggled to survive under rising operating expenses.

In Nigeria’s largely informal economy, fuel prices affect nearly every aspect of daily life. Traders transporting goods to markets, motorcycle taxi operators, farmers moving produce, and low-income workers commuting long distances all depend on affordable petrol. A sharp rise in fuel costs immediately reduces purchasing power and pushes millions of households deeper into economic hardship.

Another major source of vulnerability lies in Nigeria’s system of forward sales contracts for crude oil.

These contracts allow the government to receive immediate financing by pledging future crude production at pre-agreed prices. While the arrangement provides short-term liquidity, it also limits Nigeria’s ability to benefit from future price increases. By 2025, obligations tied to these agreements had reportedly reached trillions of naira.

As global oil prices soared during the Hormuz crisis, Nigeria could not fully capitalize on the windfall because much of its crude had already been committed under earlier agreements at lower prices. Even worse, these commitments reduced the amount of crude available for domestic refining.

The result was a damaging paradox: Nigeria possessed valuable crude oil during a global energy crisis but lacked the flexibility to use that resource to stabilize its own economy.

This problem goes beyond economics. It raises fundamental questions about governance and sovereignty. A nation unable to prioritize domestic energy security during a global crisis remains vulnerable regardless of its natural resource wealth. Nigeria’s experience demonstrates how resource-rich countries can still suffer from dependency when policy frameworks prioritize short-term financial arrangements over long-term resilience.

The crisis also spread beyond the energy sector. Disruptions in Middle Eastern petrochemical and fertilizer exports created shortages and rising prices across global agricultural markets. Nigeria’s farmers, already facing inflation and weak purchasing power, struggled with sharply higher fertilizer costs.

Smallholder farmers, who produce most of the country’s food supply, faced a severe double burden of rising production costs and declining consumer demand. Food inflation accelerated further, worsening living conditions for millions of Nigerians.

Industries dependent on petrochemicals, including plastics, textiles, and manufacturing, were also affected. Rising input costs forced some factories to reduce production while others shut down entirely. These disruptions intensified unemployment pressures and deepened broader economic instability.

The human impact of the crisis was visible everywhere: overcrowded buses charging higher fares, families reducing meals, struggling farmers delaying planting seasons, and small businesses collapsing under mounting costs.

Yet the crisis also provides an opportunity for serious reform.

First, Nigeria must guarantee domestic crude supply for local refineries. No country can claim genuine energy security while its refineries struggle to access crude produced within its own borders. The government should establish enforceable policies requiring a fixed percentage of locally produced crude to be supplied to domestic refiners under stable pricing arrangements.

Second, the forward sales contract system requires major reform. While such agreements can provide temporary financial relief, excessive dependence on future oil pledges weakens long-term economic flexibility. Nigeria should impose stricter limits on how much future production can be committed and improve transparency surrounding these contracts.

Creating strategic crude reserves would also help shield domestic refining operations from future global disruptions. Many countries maintain such reserves precisely to protect against geopolitical crises and supply shocks.

Third, Nigeria must accelerate energy diversification. The country possesses some of Africa’s largest natural gas reserves, yet these resources remain underutilized. Expanding gas-to-power infrastructure could reduce dependence on imported fuel while improving electricity supply for homes and industries.

Investment in renewable energy, especially solar power, is equally important. Diversification is no longer simply an environmental goal; it is increasingly essential for economic security and resilience against volatile global fuel markets.

Agricultural resilience must also become a national priority. Fertilizer subsidies and emergency farm support programs should be designed to protect farmers during international supply disruptions. In today’s world, food security and energy security are deeply interconnected because modern agriculture relies heavily on petrochemical inputs.

Nigeria should also use its influence within Africa to encourage regional cooperation on refining, petrochemical production, and supply chains. African economies remain highly vulnerable to disruptions originating far beyond the continent. Greater regional industrial integration could reduce dependency on distant suppliers and strengthen economic stability.

The Hormuz crisis did not create Nigeria’s energy problems. It simply exposed weaknesses that have existed for decades. Policy inconsistency, underinvestment, structural dependency, and short-term financial priorities have all contributed to the country’s vulnerability.

The Dangote Refinery remains a powerful symbol of Nigeria’s industrial potential. At the same time, it highlights the limitations of private-sector ambition within a difficult policy environment. Infrastructure alone cannot solve systemic governance failures.

Global geopolitical instability is unlikely to disappear. Energy shocks and supply disruptions will continue shaping international markets in the years ahead. Nigeria’s challenge is not merely surviving the next crisis, but building a stronger and more resilient system capable of protecting its population from future external shocks.

Nigeria possesses the resources, industrial capacity, and regional influence necessary to achieve genuine energy security. The unanswered question is whether the country’s political leadership is prepared to implement the difficult reforms required to turn resource wealth into lasting national resilience.

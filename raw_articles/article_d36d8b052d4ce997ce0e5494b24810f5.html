Air Travel Poised To Become More Expensive This Summer

Facebook Twitter LinkedIn Email
The Wealth Advisor Contributor
May 7, 2026

Air travel is poised to become significantly more expensive this summer, adding another layer of inflationary pressure that wealth advisors and RIAs should monitor closely as geopolitical instability ripples through global energy markets.

Jet fuel prices have surged more than 100% since the onset of the Iran conflict, while supply disruptions tied to the Strait of Hormuz have forced airlines worldwide to reduce capacity and cut thousands of scheduled flights. According to the US Travel Association, airline fares in March climbed 14.9% year over year, signaling that higher operating costs are already being passed through to consumers.

At the center of the disruption is the Strait of Hormuz, one of the world’s most strategically important energy corridors. JPMorgan estimates that the near-closure of the passage has effectively removed more than 13 million barrels of crude oil from global markets, creating supply shortages for refineries that convert crude into refined products such as gasoline, diesel, and aviation fuel.

Patrick De Haan, head of petroleum analysis at GasBuddy, noted that aviation fuel tends to feel the impact of refinery tightening earlier than other refined products. Jet fuel represents the smallest share among the “big three” refined outputs — gasoline, diesel, and jet fuel — which means refiners often prioritize gasoline and diesel production because of their importance to freight transportation, agriculture, and broader economic activity.

The result is a disproportionate squeeze on aviation fuel availability precisely as peak summer travel demand accelerates.

The disruption at the Strait of Hormuz has reportedly impacted nearly 20% of global seaborne jet fuel and kerosene flows. For the airline industry, this creates a dual supply shock: constrained crude availability for refineries and interrupted transportation routes for already refined fuel destined for major international airports.

GasBuddy estimates that disruptions tied to the Strait could reduce global jet fuel and kerosene supplies by approximately 620,000 barrels per day during the second quarter of 2026. The shortfall reflects both logistical interruptions and declining refinery throughput in Asia, where many refining operations remain heavily dependent on Middle Eastern crude oil imports.

For institutional investors and advisors evaluating transportation, travel, and consumer discretionary exposure, the implications are substantial. Fuel is traditionally one of the largest operating expenses for airlines, and rapid increases in energy prices can materially pressure margins, especially for carriers operating with limited hedging protection or highly competitive pricing structures.

Benchmark jet fuel swap prices along the US Gulf Coast — a key pricing metric for airline fuel procurement — remain dramatically elevated despite modest retracement from recent highs. According to Bloomberg data, prices are still roughly 50% above pre-conflict levels, trading above $330 per gallon compared with approximately $234 per gallon one month earlier.

Major carriers are already quantifying the financial impact.

Delta Air Lines projected an additional $2 billion in second-quarter fuel-related costs stemming from the recent spike in jet fuel prices. American Airlines expects approximately $4 billion in incremental fuel expenses over the course of the full year.

In response, airlines are moving aggressively to protect profitability through capacity reductions, fare increases, and route optimization.

Delta CEO Ed Bastian stated during a recent earnings call that the company is “meaningfully reducing capacity” in the current quarter while maintaining a cautious outlook until fuel markets stabilize. He also emphasized the broader financial strain facing the industry, noting that many carriers are still struggling to generate returns above their cost of capital.

Bastian characterized elevated fuel prices as a catalyst for industry consolidation and operational restructuring, warning that weaker operators may be forced to rationalize routes, merge, or exit the market altogether if current pricing conditions persist.

The timing is especially challenging given the seasonal increase in leisure and business travel demand during the summer months. Airlines are entering one of their busiest travel periods with limited operational flexibility and sharply rising input costs, leaving little margin for execution errors.

The International Energy Agency has also raised concerns about the durability of global aviation fuel inventories. Last month, IEA Executive Director Fatih Birol warned that Europe held only about six weeks of jet fuel supply under prevailing consumption trends, underscoring the fragility of current energy logistics networks.

Several major international carriers have already responded with significant schedule reductions. Lufthansa recently announced plans to cut approximately 20,000 flights through October as part of broader fuel conservation efforts and operational adjustments.

In the United States, major airlines including United Airlines, Delta, and American Airlines have scaled back service on selected routes while simultaneously implementing fuel surcharges and fare increases to offset escalating operating costs.

Delta executives recently acknowledged at an investor conference hosted by JPMorgan that fare increases are becoming necessary to preserve margins in the face of sustained fuel inflation. The comments reflect a broader industry consensus that higher ticket prices are unlikely to be temporary if energy markets remain disrupted.

For wealth advisors and RIAs, the current environment presents several important portfolio and planning considerations.

First, sustained increases in airfare and travel-related expenses may contribute to broader consumer inflation persistence, particularly within services categories. While goods inflation has moderated in several areas over the past year, energy-driven transportation costs could complicate the disinflation narrative that many investors and policymakers had expected heading into 2026.

Second, elevated fuel prices may place renewed pressure on consumer discretionary spending patterns. Higher airfare, lodging, and transportation costs could reduce household flexibility for other discretionary purchases, potentially affecting sectors tied to retail, entertainment, and leisure consumption.

Third, the airline industry itself may experience widening performance dispersion between financially stronger carriers and highly leveraged operators. Airlines with robust balance sheets, diversified route networks, and effective fuel hedging strategies may be better positioned to navigate prolonged volatility than smaller or lower-margin competitors.

Recent developments surrounding Spirit Airlines illustrate those risks. The low-cost carrier recently collapsed after prolonged financial strain and restructuring challenges, with rising fuel expenses compounding operational pressures during the period leading up to its failure.

The situation also highlights broader geopolitical risks embedded within energy markets. The Strait of Hormuz remains one of the world’s most critical maritime chokepoints, handling a substantial share of global crude oil and refined petroleum shipments. Any prolonged disruption carries implications far beyond the aviation industry, potentially affecting transportation, manufacturing, inflation expectations, and global economic growth.

Energy market volatility has historically acted as both an inflationary driver and a confidence shock for financial markets. Advisors may therefore want to evaluate client exposure across sectors sensitive to fuel costs, including airlines, logistics providers, industrial manufacturers, cruise operators, and consumer travel businesses.

At the same time, higher energy prices could create relative strength opportunities within select energy producers, midstream infrastructure operators, and commodity-linked investments that benefit from tighter global supply conditions.

From a macroeconomic standpoint, persistent energy inflation may also complicate the Federal Reserve’s policy trajectory. If transportation and energy costs continue feeding into broader consumer prices, policymakers could face additional challenges balancing inflation control against slowing economic activity.

For affluent households and business owners, rising travel costs may increasingly influence budgeting decisions, corporate travel policies, and discretionary spending plans over the coming quarters. Luxury travel demand has remained relatively resilient in recent years, but even higher-income consumers may begin adjusting behavior if airfare inflation persists alongside broader market uncertainty.

The evolving aviation fuel crisis underscores how quickly geopolitical disruptions can transmit through global supply chains and into everyday consumer expenses. For advisors, the situation serves as another reminder that energy markets remain deeply interconnected with inflation dynamics, corporate profitability, and portfolio risk management.

As airlines continue reducing capacity and passing higher fuel expenses onto travelers, the summer travel season may become an important real-time indicator of both consumer resilience and the broader economic effects of sustained commodity market disruption.

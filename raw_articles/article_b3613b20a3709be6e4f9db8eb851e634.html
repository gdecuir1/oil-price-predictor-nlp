Oil price charts produced a pattern not seen in 36 years. What happened last time?

The 50-day moving average for Brent futures topped $100 for the first time in more than four years, and is the farthest above the 200-DMA since 1990

By

Tomi Kilgore

The recent rally in oil prices may feel unique, but one technical measure says it’s only been 36 years since the last time they’ve rallied so far so fast.

When you see a move on the charts that reaches such historical extremes, many may be quick to believe that means the rally is near an end. The problem is, there’s no way of knowing how close “near” is. The only thing one can do is check what happened the last time a similar pattern appeared, and then assess whether the current situation looks set to follow the same path.

If the past acts as prologue, the bad news is that oil prices likely haven’t peaked. But the good news is, the top may be just a matter of weeks away, rather than months.

On Monday, Brent crude futures

rallied 3.4%, as the Iran peace process appears to have stalled. While prices were 8.5% below the four-year high reached a week ago, they were still up 43.7% since the Iran war started, and 72% above where they were at the end of 2025.

That has pushed the 50-day moving average to $100.01, the first time the widely followed short-term trend tracker crossed above the triple-digit threshold since March 29, 2022, which was about a month after Russia invaded Ukraine. But perhaps more significantly, the 50-DMA is now 35.2% above the 200-day moving average, using the 200-DMA as the denominator. That’s the most the 50-DMA was above the 200-DMA since Dec. 12, 1990, which was about four months after Iraq invaded Kuwait, based on a MarketWatch analysis of FactSet data.

The International Energy Agency has said that the latest Middle East conflict has triggered the largest supply disruption in history. That may be the case, but luckily for those watching the crude oil markets for guidance, there is precedent to look toward.

So what does the past tell us about where oil prices might go over the next several months?

Unfortunately for those watching gasoline prices in the U.S. keep climbing ahead of the summer travel season, there’s reason to believe the growing chorus of oil market experts who say prices will remain elevated for months, even if the Iran war ends very soon.

Read: Why crude prices won’t fall back to levels seen before the Iran war anytime soon.

In early 1990, Brent futures prices had fallen to fresh lows as the U.S. in the midst of a recession. They started climbing that July amid worries about Middle East tensions, then soared as Iraq invaded Kuwait. At that time, the 50-DMA first rose more than 35% above the 200-DMA on Sept. 27, 1990.

The spread didn’t peak until Nov. 9, 1990, which it reached 49.5%. It didn’t drop below the 35% level for the first time until that Dec. 13.

So the 50-DMA stayed more than 35% above the 200-DMA for about 2½ months. But from the time it first reached that threshold to the peak in prices, Brent futures rose less than 5% in about two weeks. By the time the spread reached its peak, oil prices had already been selling off, and were 16.6% below the $40.15 high reached on Oct. 9, 1990.

Another technical aspect was that the Relative Strength Index, a closely watched measure of momentum, had already been rising well before the start of the war, and started trending lower well before prices actually peaked. That suggested bulls were already running out of steam before they finished the job.

There are a few technical similarities between the current situation and that of 1990.

Brent futures prices had hit a multiyear low in December 2025, and were trading below the 50-DMA, which was below the 200-DMA just a few months ago. Also like 1990, prices started rising before the war started, and were already above the 200-DMA when hostilities began.

And notice how the RSI had peaked soon after the war started, and well before prices reached their top. Look familiar?

Of course, statisticians would scoff at using one past episode to try to make a prediction about oil prices 36 years later.

Walter Zimmerman, chief technical strategist at ICAP Technical Analysis, sees reason to believe the latest price pattern for Brent is just a consolidation before “an acceleration higher” kicks in.

Zimmerman believes the next target for prices is just under $150. That’s basically what Morgan Stanley warned would happen by summer if peace isn’t declared soon.

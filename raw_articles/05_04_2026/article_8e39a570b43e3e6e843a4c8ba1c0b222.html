UAE Exits OPEC+ as Strait Blockade Nullifies Production Increase

OPEC+ Agrees to Raise Output, but Blocked Hormuz Strait Halts Exports, Experts Warn of Prolonged High Prices

By Park Jeong-hoon
Published 2026.05.05. 00:52

The United Arab Emirates (UAE) has withdrawn from the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) and its expanded coalition OPEC+, followed by its exit from the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries (OAPEC). While Saudi Arabia-led OPEC+ responded with a production increase, the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz has halted crude exports, leading market observers to label the move a "declarative measure."

The UAE officially announced its withdrawal from OAPEC on the 3rd (local time), after leaving OPEC and OPEC+ starting on the 1st. Established in 1968, OAPEC is a consultative body for Arab oil-producing nations, including Saudi Arabia and Kuwait.

The UAE’s actions stem from long-standing grievances exacerbated by the war. Despite having a production capacity of 4.85 million barrels per day, the UAE was restricted to 3.6 million barrels due to OPEC+ quotas. Meanwhile, Iraq and Russia’s repeated quota overruns were largely tolerated. Amid Iran’s attacks on UAE territory, Gulf allies remained ineffective, and with exports blocked under quotas, the UAE pursued an independent path. The existence of Fujairah Port outside the Strait of Hormuz is also cited as a factor enabling its exit.

OPEC+ swiftly responded. On the 3rd, seven major OPEC+ members, including Saudi Arabia and Russia, agreed to increase June crude production by 188,000 barrels per day. However, with the strait blocked, both the UAE’s unilateral production hike and OPEC+’s announcement have limited practical impact. Before the war, the Strait of Hormuz handled an average of 20 million barrels daily, accounting for roughly 20% of global oil and petroleum product supplies. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), even utilizing alternative routes like Saudi Arabia’s Red Sea passage and the UAE’s Fujairah Port pipeline, replacement capacity remains at only a quarter of pre-war Hormuz volumes.

A more fundamental issue is that even if the strait reopens, market normalization will be challenging. Post-reopening, mine clearance, vessel repositioning, stabilizing soaring insurance costs, and restarting oil wells and refineries will require complex recovery processes. In its latest issue (May 2nd edition), the British *Economist* noted, “Markets and oil futures prices are overly optimistic, assuming this shock will soon resolve. Political reopening and physical normalization are entirely different matters.” It added, “Even if the strait reopens, it could take months for fuel to stably reach refineries and gas stations.” Indeed, Asian petrochemical firms have reduced operational rates to 60–75% due to naphtha shortages, with some halting production. Concerns over diesel and jet fuel inventory shortages are also growing in Europe.

Domestic experts emphasize the likelihood of prolonged high oil prices. Professor Seok Byung-hoon of Ewha Womans University stated, “Oil-producing nations inside the strait face production disruptions due to saturated storage facilities, making a swift return to pre-war operations difficult even if the strait reopens. The scale of OPEC+’s production increase is also insufficient to alter global oil price trends.” Dr. Jang Tae-hoon of the Korea Energy Economics Institute noted, “Even if the strait reopens, vessels will exit sequentially, and restarting halted facilities could take over a month. As long as geopolitical risks persist in the strait, oil prices are unlikely to return to the pre-war $70 range soon.” Dr. Jang added, “If a production ‘chicken game’ between the UAE and OPEC begins after the crisis, downward pressure on oil prices could strengthen. However, if the strait remains closed beyond June, high oil prices are likely to extend into next year.”

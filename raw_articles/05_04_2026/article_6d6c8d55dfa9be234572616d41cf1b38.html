Oil and product prices set to be ‘higher for longer’ as Hormuz remains closed

Oil markets
By Paul Hodges on 03-May-2026
By Paul Hodges on 03-May-2026
SHARE THIS

Oil Soars to Wartime High

Oil and product prices are set to be ‘higher for longer’ as the Strait of Hormuz remains closed. And slowly but surely, markets are starting to adapt to this new world:

  • Until the end of February, everyone had expected oil and product prices would continue to fall
  • Oil markets were expecting a 4.25mbd surplus in Q1, even with strong heating demand
  • Brent prices tumbled to $59/bbl in December as the surplus began to impact
  • But since then, prices have doubled as the chart shows

Oil markets are like a supertanker It takes a relatively long time for them to adjust to new conditions. And so it is only now that the downstream market is beginning to reflect the new reality.

Higher oil prices will mean higher inflation

Oil prices have now doubled since December. But most economic commentators are only now waking up to this new reality:

  • Last week, for example, the Bank of England published its latest oil price forecast
  • Its preferred Scenario B suggested prices would peak at $108/bbl
  • Unfortunately for the Bank, the oil price rose to $126/bbl on the day of publication

It now faces the need to move to its Scenario C – which suggests oil peaks at >$200/bbl.

The issue, as the chart highlights, is that these higher prices are now starting to impact downstream value chains:

  • Transport costs have already soared for gasoline, diesel, jet fuel and bunker fuel
  • These prices are now working their way into a wide range of consumer products
  • Polymer prices, for example, have been jumping 20%-30% per month since March
  • Pharmaceutical costs are also rising – raw material costs for paracetamol are up 400%

Oil and natural gas are now physically short

Equally worrying is that the world is now actually short of oil and gas for the first time since 1979.

World oil demand/supply balance quarterly

A month ago, the International Energy Agency highlighted that ‘demand destruction‘ was now inevitable:

“This crisis, as it stands now, is two oil crises and one gas crisis put all together.”

This can only happen in one of two ways:

  • Prices rocket higher till the necessary number of people can no longer afford to buy
  • Governments ban or restrict demand – by banning Sunday driving or cutting speed limits

Asian governments have already imposed such measures. Most cannot afford to pay today’s prices, so they have to cutback.

But this will be a new experience for most people in the West. They weren’t alive in the 1970s when these measures were last needed. For them it will be a major shock.

But as the IEA’s latest Report shows, we are now moving into their ‘Protracted Case‘ scenario.

In this Scenario, the world is physically short of oil. The red line showing demand goes negative, as the chart shows.

Product cycles and lead times vary by industry

Product cycles and lead times vary by industry

The next few weeks are likely to be the moment when this ‘oil and gas price shock’ starts to become real for most people.

We have mostly forgotten that industries have their own cycles and lead times, as the chart shows:

  • In Silicon Valley, everything moves quickly – lead and cycle times are relatively short
  • But in Napa Valley, things move slowly – it takes time to plant the vines and mature the wine

Electronics are a Silicon Valley industry – short-term thinking dominates because cycle times are so short.

But oil and chemicals are Napa Valley industries. There are no quick solutions.

As with Covid in 2020, a ‘wake-up call’ is now about to hit most Western governments.

Critical products are at increasing risk of becoming unaffordable, or unobtainable.
